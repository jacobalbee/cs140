Early Life and Conversion to the Church

Brigham Young was born in Whitingham, Vermont on June 1, 1801, the ninth of 11 siblings. At the time of his birth, his parents, John and Abigail Young, were devout members of the Methodist Church. His father had a very interesting history of his own, having been made an orphan at age six when Brigham's grandfather was killed in a fatal accident. After the accident, John and his brother were bound out under a contract of servitude to a wealthy landowner in New Hampshire. At age 16, John enlisted in the Revolutionary War and served under the command of General George Washington. Once the war was over, he married and became a farmer in Vermont. In 1804, however, three years after Brigham's birth, the Young family moved to western New York, where they resided for several decades.

As early as 1801, when Brigham was born, his mother, Abigail, contracted tuberculosis. For all of the time that Brigham knew his mother, she battled the disease, eventually dying when he was just 14 years old. Brigham's childhood was one of hard labor and little money, with almost no schooling. Brigham helped his father and brothers clear land, farm, and do household chores. He learned to read and write at home, but he was only ever given 11 days of formal education, under a traveling schoolmaster. From his youth, Brigham was taught from the Bible and the family was raised on the tenets of the Methodist faith, yet Brigham delayed officially joining any particular denomination until adulthood out of uncertainty. In the meantime, he frequently attended religious revivals. At the age of 16, Brigham's father sent him off to pursue a trade, and he apprenticed in Auburn, New York under a cabinetmaker. During this period of his life, Brigham had several, related occupations: he worked as a carpenter, joiner, painter, and glazier. He quickly became known for his furniture and for ornately carved mantelpieces. Eventually, he moved from Auburn to Port Byron, New York, in the spring of 1823, where he repaired furniture and painted canal boats. In October of 1824, he and his first wife, Miriam Angeline Works, were married in Aurelius, Cayuga County, New York. At the age of 23, Brigham formally joined the Methodist Church with his wife, but he apparently still had reservations with their practices, since he requested to be baptized by immersion, in contrast to the usual Methodist practice of sprinkling. He noted that, although they didn't have all the answers to his religious questions, he felt that his conversion to Methodism might lead him to live a better life. Brigham and Miriam eventually had two daughters together: Elizabeth, born in 1825 in Port Byron, and Vilate, born in 1830, after the family had moved to Mendon, New York, to be near Brigham's father and other relatives. In Mendon, Brigham worked as a carpenter and joiner and built and operated a mill.

In the spring of 1830, Brigham was first introduced to the Book of Mormon, through his brother Phinehas, who had accepted a copy while preaching on behalf of the Reformed Methodist Church and who had come to believe in its truth. His brother Joseph and his father John were also quickly interested in the new scripture. None of the members of the Young family joined the Church in 1830, however. Despite their new testimonies of the Book of Mormon, many of them still sought to reconcile their belief in the book with their membership in the Reformed Methodist Church.

In the fall of 1831, Latter-day Saint Elders arrived in Mendon, New York, where Brigham was residing, and began to preach the gospel. Brigham attended their preaching and felt compelled to believe. He later wrote: "I could easily out-talk them, though I had never preached; but their testimony was like fire in my bones." Several months later, in January of 1832, he accompanied his brother Phinehas and his friend Heber C. Kimball to Columbia, Pennsylvania, where a branch of the Church had been established. The trio travelled through snow and ice by sleigh, crossing rivers and braving the cold weather. They were driven forward by a desire to learn more about the principles of Mormonism. After arriving in Columbia, they stayed with the congregation there for one week, before returning home, even more convinced of the truth of the Restoration. Almost immediately after this experience, Brigham rode northward to find his brother Joseph, who was then a traveling preacher for the Methodist Church. He explained what he had learned to his brother and, just a few weeks later, in April 1832, his brother Joseph, his father, John Young, his stepmother Hannah, his sister Fanny and her husband Roswell Murray, his sister Rhoda and her husband John Greene, his brother Phinehas and his wife Clarissa, his brother Lorenzo, and himself all joined The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, which had just under 3,000 members at the end of that year.

Brigham himself was baptized and confirmed on April 15, 1832 in his own mill stream by Elder Eleazer Miller. In the same day that he was baptized, he was ordained an Elder in the Church. His wife, Miriam, was baptized and confirmed just three weeks later.

Kirtland, Ohio

Sometime around her baptism (either shortly before or shortly after), Miriam Young contracted tuberculosis, the same disease that had previously killed Brigham's mother. By late summer of 1832, she had become bedridden and Brigham took over most of the housework, working for low wages, preparing breakfast, dressing his daughters, and cleaning the home. By early September, Miriam had unfortunately passed away. In her last moments, Brigham recorded that she clapped and praised God and called on all around her to do the same. With the death of his wife and with two daughters to care for, Brigham moved into the home of his friend and neighbor Heber C. Kimball and his wife Vilate.

About three weeks after Miriam's death, Brigham, his brother Joseph, and his friend Heber traveled together to visit Kirtland, Ohio, and, there, met the Prophet Joseph Smith for the first time. Their first meeting was rather mundane; the Prophet was in the middle of chopping and hauling wood, but he paused to shake Brigham's hand, and invited him to help. Despite the apparent casualness of the meeting, Brigham recorded that it was at this occasion that he received a sure testimony, by the spirit of prophecy, that Joseph was called of God.

That evening, when Brigham and some of the Saints had gathered together in Joseph Smith's home, Brigham was called on to pray, and, in this prayer, the Spirit worked upon him and he began speaking in an unknown language. When questioned by some in attendance whether or not the outburst was of God, the Prophet Joseph Smith proclaimed that it was a divine gift and that Brigham had spoken in the pure Adamic tongue. Shortly after Brigham had left, Joseph Smith reportedly prophesied that Brigham Young would one day preside over the Church.

Young soon became a missionary for the Church, preaching in West Loughborough, Canada with his brother Joseph for about two months, from December 1832 to February 1833. During this first mission to Canada, the Young brothers baptized about 45 people and organized several branches. They returned home for a couple months, then set out for Canada again, revisiting the branches near West Loughborough and then continuing onwards to neighboring regions, preaching and baptizing along the way for about four months.

Brigham then returned to Mendon in August 1833, after this second mission to Canada, and, in accordance to the counsel of the Prophet Joseph Smith, made preparations to gather himself and his two daughters to Kirtland, Ohio with the rest of the Saints. In Kirtland, Brigham worked tirelessly to build up the Kingdom of Zion, applying his training in carpentry, plastery, and glazing to the construction of homes, public buildings, and the Kirtland Temple. In February of 1834, Brigham, who had since the death of his first wife been a single father, married Mary Ann Angel, who quickly adopted his children as her own.

Under Joseph Smith's direction, Brigham and his brother Joseph marched to Missouri with about 200 armed men in Zion's Camp in the spring of 1834. The militia intended to help the Saints driven out of Jackson County by vigilante mobs and, if possible, restore them to their lands. By most counts, about 1,200 Saints had been driven out at gunpoint from the site which revelation had designated as the place where the New Jerusalem would be built. Intending to defend these religious refugees, the camp covered up to 40 miles a day at times and roughly 900-1000 miles in all. Brigham carried with him a gun, bayonet, and ammunition. They arrived in Clay County, Missouri in early June, but the expedition soon encountered a severe outbreak of cholera, infecting around 68 of the brethren, including the Prophet Joseph Smith, and killing at least 13. The camp's presence stirred up tension in Missouri but it never fought the mobs head-on. Contrary to the purposes of the expedition, the state of Missouri declined to restore the Saints who had been pushed out of Jackson County, and the camp was finally discharged on July 3, 1834. While the primary purpose of the expedition was never realized, it served as a testing and training ground, from which nine of the first Twelve Apostles were chosen.

After returning to Kirtland, Brigham continued his day job, supporting his family and community by quarrying rock, working on the Kirtland Temple, and finishing the city printing office and schoolroom. On February 14th of 1835, Joseph Smith called a council of elders together and unfolded yet another aspect of the ongoing Restoration of the Church. In accordance with New Testament practice, the Prophet created the Quorum of Twelve Apostles, selecting Brigham Young as the second Apostle of the Church, following Elder Lyman E. Johnson. The first Quorum of the Seventy was organized two weeks later, on February 28. On May 2, Brigham was called by the Prophet to go and preach the gospel to the Native Americans: the seed of Joseph and descendants of Lehi. On the 4th of May, Brigham traveled with the Twelve to Dunkirk, New York, then Westfield, where the Twelve held a conference before splitting into pairs and moving eastward to preach the gospel and visit the branches. At a conference in Freedom, New York, Brigham was set apart for a special mission to the Seneca reservation in Cattaraugas County. With two other elders, Brigham preached to the Seneca people but no baptisms were recorded from this trip. Brigham continued onwards to preach in portions of New England before returning to Kirtland in September.

Back in Kirtland, Brigham labored again on the Temple and also attended the Hebrew school taught by Jewish scholar Joshua Seixas. At the dedication of the Kirtland Temple, Brigham and the rest of the Apostles received their washings and anointings and the ordinance of washing of feet.

In the spring of 1836, Brigham and his brother Joseph set off on yet another mission, preaching across New York and New England. Upon his return, apostasy began to tear at the Church in Kirtland. The failure of the Kirtland Safety Society led to open revolt among many of the top leaders of the Church, including several Apostles and Book of Mormon witnesses. At this time, Brigham displayed incredible loyalty to the Prophet, proclaiming in the face of dissenters that their slanderings could not destroy the appointment of the Prophet and could only destroy their own authority.

In 1837, Brigham went on two additional mission trips in New York and New England, from March to May and from June to August. Finally, on December 22nd of that year, he and his family were forced to leave Kirtland due to threats from apostates and dissenters. They joined his brother Lorenzo in Dublin, Indiana, where they stayed for a while before traveling southwest to Far West, Missouri, arriving in March of 1838.

Missouri

Tensions with the old, predominantly Southern settlers of Missouri grew stronger as Latter-day Saint refugees flooded in from Kirtland. Brigham was present in Missouri during the 1838 Mormon War, in which violent Missourian mobs sieged the Latter-day Saint town of De Witt and then continued their campaign into Daviess County. Governor Lilburn Boggs refused to intervene, leaving the Latter-day Saints to defend themselves, mobilizing the Caldwell County militia and the Danite organization to launch retaliatory raids. Following the Battle of Crooked River, in which a Latter-day Saint rescue party, formed by David W. Patten to free three captives clashed with a Ray County militia company, resulting in the deaths of three Latter-day Saints and one Missouri militiaman. On October 27th, Governor Lilburn Boggs issued Executive Order 44, calling for the Saints to be "exterminated or driven from the State." Shortly following this order, an unauthorized force of about 240 militia men descended upon the Latter-day Saint settlement of Haun's Mill, killing 17 Saints, including multiple children, and injuring 15. On this same day, the exterminating army of Governor Boggs, commanded by General Lucas, arrived at the settlement of Far West, having come to strengthen and protect the mobs of anti-Mormons. Brigham recorded that he observed their approach, initially hopeful that they had come to protect the Saints. However, it was soon clear that the opposite was true. He recorded: "The next morning General Lucas demanded and took away the arms of the Militia of Caldwell County ... so soon as they obtained possession of the army, they commenced their ravages by plundering the citizens of their bedding, clothing, money, wearing apparel, and every thing of value they could lay their hands upon, and also attempting to violate the chastity of the women in sight of their husbands and friends, under the pretense of hunting for prisoners and arms. The soldiers shot down our oxen, cows, hogs and fowls, at our own doors, taking part away and leaving the rest to rot in the streets."

The Saints, including Brigham Young, were forced to flee from the state. While Joseph Smith and his counselors were held in Liberty Jail from December 1838 to spring of 1839, Brigham Young, as seniormost Apostle following Thomas B. Marsh's exodus from the Church in October, was suddenly the de facto leader of the Saints. He organized the exodus from the state, fund-raising where necessary to provide safe passage to the poor. On February 14th, 1839, Young and his family left for Illinois, eventually ending up in Quincy, where he was joined by many of the refugees, including several of those who had witnessed the horror at Haun's Mill. On April 18th, to fulfill a commandment given by the Lord in Doctrine & Covenants 118, which stated that the Twelve must hold a conference in Far West, appoint new Church leaders, and then take leave of the Saints there on April 26th of 1839. On the morning of April 26th, they did all that the Lord commanded them, holding a conference, ordaining two new apostles (Wilford Woodruff and George A. Smith), ordained two men to the Seventy, rolled a large stone into place at the site of the Far West Temple to "rebuild its foundation", and then bade farewell to a small group of Saints that still remained in Far West.

They then rode several miles away from town and oversaw the removal of the final members of the Church from Far West. Brigham soon after returned to Quincy, where he met up with Joseph Smith, who had escaped from prison. In the month of May, under the direction of the Prophet, Brigham moved his family northward to Montrose, Iowa, where they resided in a room in the old military barracks, across the Mississippi from Commerce, Illinois, which would later become Nauvoo.

Nauvoo, Illinois

After many months helping his family settle in Montrose, Brigham joined most of his fellow Apostles on a mission to England. He traveled across the Eastern States to New York City, preaching along the way. From NYC, the Apostles departed to England, where they wandered the countryside, testifying of the Restored Gospel. The entire mission, including the travel to and from England took about 22 months. Brigham left Montrose on September 14, 1839, and returned to Nauvoo on July 1, 1841. While in England, the Twelve baptized between seven and eight thousand people, established branches in nearly every notable British town, printed 5,000 Books of Mormon, and directed the first emigrant companies to Nauvoo from England. On April 14, 1840, while he was still in England, Brigham Young was formally sustained as president of the quorum.

Brigham Young's long mission away from his family had taken quite the toll on his wife, Mary Ann, who had suffered illness and poverty. Despite the challenges, she had encouraged him to fulfill his duties to the Lord and told him that they would be blessed if he did so. The revelation that was then received through the Prophet Joseph Smith must have been a welcome message, then, because, on July 9th, Joseph stated that the Lord had accepted his sacrifice and offering and that he would no longer be required to travel and was instead to focus on taking "special care" of his family. Brigham was soon elected a member of the Nauvoo city council. During this period of his life, he and the Twelve took charge of many Church matters, including officiating baptisms for the dead at the newly-dedicated Nauvoo Temple baptismal font. On May 4th, 1842, Brigham Young received his washings, anointings, and endowments in Joseph Smith's private office, becoming one of the first members of the Anointed Quorum.

On May 26th, 1843, the Prophet Joseph Smith introduced to Brigham the doctrine of the new and everlasting covenant. He was sealed by the authority of the priesthood to his second wife, Mary Ann, just a couple days later, on May 29th.

It was also in Nauvoo that Brigham Young was first introduced by the Prophet Joseph Smith to the subject of plural marriage. He stated that, when Joseph revealed the practice to him, it was the first time in his life that he had desired the grave. Though Brigham never described explicitly why, he did mention his anxieties that, if he were to take a second wife and then apostasize, he would leave his plural family "worse off." Nonetheless, he prayed unceasingly and exercised faith and received a testimony by the power of the Holy Ghost that the practice was a commandment from God. On June 14, 1842, he took a second wife, Lucy Ann Decker, who was followed by Augusta Adams and Harriet Cook between 1842 and 1843, and Clarissa Decker in late spring, 1844. Very little is known of Mary Ann's initial reaction to polygamy, but, at least by the time that we do have records, she had apparently endorsed the practice and gave her consent to Brigham Young's additional marriages, welcoming the new wives into the family.

While Brigham was never again called to serve another long mission as he had in England, he did serve a series of short missions during his years in Nauvoo. From September to early November 1842, he traveled through many of the Eastern States to answer slanderous charges by apostates, from July to October 1843, he served a short eastern mission to collect funds for the Nauvoo Temple, and, finally, from May 21st to August 6th, 1844, to the eastern states again to gather support for Joseph Smith's candidacy for president of the United States. Brigham was in Peterborough, New Hampshire, when he first received news of Joseph Smith's martyrdom on July 16-18, after which he hurried back to Nauvoo.
