Review of "The Antikythera Mechanism: The oldest mechanical universe in its scientific milieu" by Xenophon Moussas

Introduction

For my History & Ethics of Computing course, I chose to read the article “The Antikythera Mechanism: The oldest mechanical universe in its scientific milieu” by Xenophon Moussas. This choice was inspired by my personal fascination with the ancient world. In modern society, we often underestimate the capabilities of ancient people, and discoveries such as the Antikythera Mechanism break these underlying assumptions.

What is the Antikythera Mechanism?

As explained by Moussas in his article, the Antikythera Mechanism is a highly advanced and very complicated astronomical instrument, probably constructed between 150 to 100 BC (Moussas 135). Composed of bronze gears, the clock-like device is the oldest known analog computer and was likely used to perform various astronomical calculations (Moussas 135). The mysterious Mechanism has fascinated researchers since its discovery in 1901-2 within a 1st century BC shipwreck near the island of Antikythera (Moussas 143).

Though its complex architecture and mathematical basis may initially appear to be out-of-place in the ancient world and far ahead of its time, Moussas states that the Mechanism is “definitely not the first one of its kind” (Moussas 135). He then provides substantial background on the incredible depth of mathematical and scientific knowledge possessed by the ancient Greeks and on the distribution of similarly complex mechanical devices across the ancient Mediterranean world.

The Antikythera Mechanism itself was likely housed in an elaborately decorated box, possibly imitating a miniature Greek Temple, with automated statuettes that functioned as pointers (Moussas 135). It contained multiple concentric scales and dials, and specially arranged bronze gear trains drove the displays (Moussas 135). The Mechanism was capable of numerous calculations; it could:

  1. measure the angular distance of a celestial body from the horizon
  2. measure the angular distance between any two celestial bodies
  3. identify the Sun’s position along the ecliptic, or zodiac
  4. identify the Moon’s position and phase, including changes in its angular speed during the month
  5. predict solar and lunar eclipses
  6. identify calendar dates

Combined with reference information, the Mechanism could also be used to determine geographic latitude (Moussas 144). A train of four gears was used to model the movement of the Moon with a “good approximation of Kepler’s second law” (Moussas 143). Moussas also notes that the Mechanism may have possessed a planetary show display or planetarium (Moussas 135).

Ancient Greek Mathematics and Science

The computational power of the Antikythera Mechanism was built upon the discoveries of centuries of ancient Greek mathematicians, scientists, and astronomers. Unlike many of their contemporaries, the ancient Greeks sought to explain nature through observation, reasoning, and natural causes. Greek scientists sought to understand the laws that governed nature. Moussas quotes a Greek hymn entitled The Law as saying:

The Hymn to the Law.
I call the King of all, mortal and immortal, Celestial Law that sets the stars in
their position and motion, righteous and equitable seal, of the seas and the Earth,
[law] that never deviates or changes, and what looks uncertain in nature becomes
inescapable as it follows the laws of nature. (Moussas 137)

Convinced that the essence of nature is described by mathematics after his discovery of musical harmony, mathematician Pythagoras founded the highly important Pythagorean philosophy, which influenced later thinkers such as Plato (Moussas 138). Aristarchus determined that the earth revolved around the sun (Moussas 140), while mathematician Eudoxus modeled celestial motion using nested moving spheres, and Meton introduced a lunisolar period of 19 years: the time it takes the Moon to reappear in the same place ofthe sky withthe same phase (Moussas 139). Callipus expanded Eudoxus’ model of planetary motion and replaced Meton’s model with a more accurate 76-year cycle (Moussas 139). Finally, Hipparchus introduced eccentricity and retrograde motion into the Eudoxian system to account for inaccuracies (Moussas 140).

Mechanical Engineering in the Ancient World

The Antikythera Mechanism builds on the discoveries of each of these mathematicians, especially of Hipparchus. Its complex mechanical architecture wasn’t solely unique either. In Greek temples, automatic doors and other automatic devices existed (Moussas 140). According to the records, Archimedes constructed numerous mechanical devices, including a mechanical celestial sphere and a planetarium, as well as a very complicated clock (Moussas 140). Posidonius also possessed a mechanical planetarium, like that of Archimedes (Moussas 141). Mechanical clocks and astronomical devices are described many times in Greek literature (Moussas 141).

Overall, the Antikythera Mechanism, though it may seem complex relative to the technology of following centuries, is simply a reflection of the advanced technology and scientific understanding of the ancient Greeks.
